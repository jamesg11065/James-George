# INTERVIEW PREPARATION — BA SPEEDBIRD PILOT ACADEMY
### Competency Questions & STAR Stories · James George · July 2026

Your stories are the same proven set from the Jet2 prep — what changes is the **closing line and the company link**. BA's assessors weight **integrity, CRM/speaking up, composure, and customer focus**, and they expect real depth on BA as a business.

**BA competency themes:** Integrity & professionalism · Teamwork/CRM · Communication · Composure under pressure · Situational awareness · Customer focus · Motivation & commercial awareness

## Q1. "What does integrity mean to you?" ⭐ *Reported as a near-certain early question*

**Answer in your own words, then evidence it:**

> "Integrity is doing the right thing when it costs you something and nobody would have known otherwise. In aviation that means reporting your own errors, refusing to cut corners under time pressure, and being honest about your limits."

**Evidence — PPL pre-flight oil check:** ready for a solo nav, tight weather window, oil at the lower acceptable limit — technically legal. Flagged it, topped up, lost 20 minutes and the slot. Nobody asked me to.

**Closing line:** *"The pre-flight check is a promise you make to everyone who trusts the aircraft. I'd rather be 20 minutes late and certain than on time and second-guessing myself."*

**BA link:** Integrity is BA's headline value. A 100-year-old brand carrying 46 million passengers a year survives on exactly this: crews who choose procedure over convenience without being watched.

## Q2. "Tell me about a quick decision in a safety-critical situation."

**Lifeguarding — pool rescue (Nene Centre):** spotted a child in distress in the deep end — head low, arms thrashing, no forward movement. Three whistle blasts, entered with the rescue tube, towed the child to the edge while directing a colleague to clear the pool and stand by the EAP. Child safe; incident report and team debrief immediately after.

**Closing line:** *"The drills exist so that when the real moment arrives you don't have to think — you act. That's the mindset I want to bring to a BA flight deck."*

**BA link — professionalism & standards:** BA's training philosophy is procedures rehearsed until they're instinctive. This shows I already operate that way in a real safety-critical job.

---

## Q3. "Tell me about a time you stayed calm under pressure."

**PPL — deteriorating weather on solo nav:** cloud base dropping 15 miles from home. Aviate–navigate–communicate: straight and level, confirmed fuel and position, pan-pan to ATC, requested vectors to an alternate, landed safely. Instructor: declaring early was good airmanship, not weakness.

**Closing line:** *"Structure is what keeps you safe when pressure is real. I've tested that in the air, alone, and it held."*

**BA link — composure & communication:** BA operates into the world's most congested airspace out of Heathrow. Calm, structured radio work under pressure isn't optional — and the Stage 2 capacity test measures exactly this skill.

---

## Q4. "Tell me about working effectively in a team."

**Lifeguarding — school gala:** 200 children, four guards. A colleague had to step away; caught the team lead's eye, redistributed zones between three of us without interrupting the event, extended my scanning arc, stayed in communication at each rotation. Manager called the handover seamless.

**Closing line:** *"Good teams don't just do their own job — they stay aware of the whole team and close gaps before they become problems. That's what a multi-crew cockpit is."*

**BA link — teamwork/CRM:** BA pioneered CRM training in Europe. Crews of thousands rotate constantly — you fly with someone new almost every trip, so the instinct to integrate fast and monitor the whole operation is core.

---

## Q5. "Tell me about a time you disagreed with someone senior." ⭐ *CRM question — BA loves this one*

**PPL — instructor's routing:** briefed route ran close to a CAS boundary with cloud building to the west. Raised it specifically before walking out: "I'm not fully comfortable with the margin — could we look at a slight deviation east?" Instructor reviewed the weather and adjusted the track. He said the skill wasn't the reroute — it was raising a reasoned concern to someone senior, calmly.

**Closing line:** *"I didn't speak up because I thought I knew better. Something felt wrong, and silence would have been the wrong choice. Saying it calmly and accepting the outcome — that's what CRM actually looks like."*

**BA link:** BA explicitly trains First Officers to challenge Captains — flat-gradient flight decks are a pillar of its safety culture. This story is direct evidence you already have the instinct.

---

## Q6. "Tell me about a mistake with real consequences."

**PPL — navigation error on solo cross-country:** misidentified a checkpoint, pressed on convincing myself the landscape fit, ended up meaningfully off track with tighter fuel/time margins. Stopped rationalising, re-fixed position from two unambiguous features (railway + reservoir), gave ATC my honest position, flew a conservative direct route home. Debrief verdict: "get-there-itis in miniature." Now I verbalise checkpoints, and if something doesn't match, I stop — I don't rationalise.

**Closing line:** *"The mistake wasn't misreading the map — it was continuing rather than admitting I wasn't certain. I've never done that again. With passengers behind me, pause-and-verify is not optional."*

**BA link — just culture:** BA runs a non-punitive safety reporting system (ASRs); the operation gets safer only when pilots self-report honestly. This is that behaviour, demonstrated before anyone paid me to have it.

## Q7. "Tell me about delivering excellent customer service." ⭐ *Weighted higher at BA than most airlines*

**Lifeguarding — nervous returning swimmer:** noticed an adult gripping the lane rope, hesitant. No safety issue — but on a rotation break I checked in, learned they were returning after years away, offered a few practical suggestions, checked in again as they left. They thanked me and came back the next week.

**Closing line:** *"What people experience depends on whether anyone is paying attention. For a premium airline, that attention is the product."*

**BA link — customer focus:** BA sells premium service; its margin lives in First, Club and the transatlantic network. Pilots are part of that product — the PA that reassures a nervous cabin in turbulence is customer service at 38,000 feet.

## Q8. "Tell me about adapting when a plan changed suddenly."

**Gliding — thermal collapse at Welland:** lost planned height on a cross-country with no engine to fix it. Identified a landable field as the fallback, computed whether home was achievable with margin, committed, radioed the club early, flew a conservative circuit home.

**Closing line:** *"Rigidly following a dead plan is more dangerous than the change that killed it. Flexibility and structure aren't opposites — you need both."*

**BA link — situational awareness:** Heathrow ops are a constant stream of holds, slot changes and re-clearances. A pilot who reassesses without drama is exactly what the network needs.

**Why BA specifically:**

**Closing line:** *"I'm not here because I like the idea of flying — I've been doing it since I was 13, and I've funded every hour myself. Speedbird is the first door to a BA flight deck that opens on merit alone, and I've spent five years earning the right to walk through it."*

## The Nine Pilot Competencies — Good AND Bad Example for Each ⭐

Direct advice from a successful cadet: build a **good example and a bad example** for each of the nine ICAO pilot competencies, write them in a notebook, and get them in your head. Interviewers pull questions from this framework, and "tell me about a time you did this badly" is as likely as the positive version. A "bad" example is only bad if you can't show what changed — every one below ends in a corrected behaviour.

| # | Competency | Good example (yours) | Bad / developmental example (yours) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Application of Knowledge** | GCSE-to-PPL theory applied in real nav planning; explaining the no-diving rule via spinal-injury mechanics to the angry parent | Early PPL: relied on rote checklist knowledge without understanding *why* — the oil-limit moment taught you to reason from the underlying system, not just the number |
| 2 | **Application of Procedures & Regulations** | Oil at lower limit → refused to depart, topped up, lost the slot (integrity story) | Misjudged approach: initially tried to salvage a non-stable approach before going around — now the stable-approach criteria decide, not your ego |
| 3 | **Communication** | Pan-pan call — early, calm, structured; de-escalating the angry parent | Nav error flight: didn't tell ATC anything until *after* re-fixing position — you now report uncertainty early rather than waiting until you've solved it alone |
| 4 | **Flight Path Management — Automation** | Disciplined use of G1000-style aids in nav training; cross-checking instruments against the visual picture | Early tendency to follow the moving map instead of the chart — the misidentified checkpoint happened partly because the screen made you overconfident. Now: raw-data cross-check every checkpoint |
| 5 | **Flight Path Management — Manual Control** | Glider thermal-collapse recovery: energy management with no engine; conservative circuit flown accurately under stress | The high-and-fast final approach itself — poor energy management on the day; fixed by setting up earlier and defining a go-around gate |
| 6 | **Leadership & Teamwork** | School gala zone redistribution; bringing the quiet swimmer/colleague dynamic into play | A time you stayed in your zone too literally early in lifeguarding — did your job but didn't monitor the team; the gala taught you to scan the whole operation |
| 7 | **Problem Solving & Decision Making** | Thermal collapse: options identified before needed, committed decisively; go-around decision | Get-there-itis nav error: pressed on based on an assumption because stopping felt like defeat — now you pause and verify the moment reality stops matching the plan |
| 8 | **Situational Awareness** | Spotting the drowning child from subtle posture changes; tracking cloud building west of the CAS boundary and speaking up | Losing positional awareness on the cross-country by making the landscape fit the chart — the definitive SA failure, honestly owned, with the verbalise-checkpoints fix |
| 9 | **Workload Management** | Aviate-navigate-communicate sequencing in the deteriorating-weather flight — one problem at a time, in order | Early solo circuits: trying to do everything at once on final (radio, checks, flying) until the approach fell apart — you learned to shed low-priority tasks and fly first |

**How to use this table:** the right-hand column entries marked from real events are ready to tell. Two of them (rote knowledge early on, staying in your zone too literally, doing everything at once in early circuits) are *plausible reconstructions of typical learning moments* — before Friday, spend ten minutes replacing each with the real memory of yours that fits, so every answer is genuinely true. Write all 18 in your notebook, one page per competency, STAR bullets only.

## Q12. "Who do you think should get through from this process, and why?" *(reflective — asked at the end for at least one successful candidate)*

This is really a question about **you**: do you observe people accurately, can you praise a competitor without insecurity, and did you engage with the group at all?

- Name real candidates you spoke to (waiting room + group exercise). Cite one specific observed behaviour each.
- Frame it in competency language: "She managed the time for us without dominating — that's workload management and teamwork in one."
- Be generous and honest; never say "me", never rank yourself above others, never say "everyone".

## Delivery Notes

- **Write your answers in a notebook** (successful cadet's method) — the act of writing them fixes them in your head far better than re-reading a document. One page per competency: good example, bad example, STAR bullets.
- 90 seconds per story; spend most of it on **Action**. Pause, then give the closing line as your own thought — never recited.
- Drop **one** fact per answer (£2.23bn profit, 160 places, 10% SAF by 2030) — one lands as awareness, three sounds like Wikipedia.
- If asked something with no prepared story: pause, say "Let me pick the best example," and structure it STAR out loud. Composure in that moment *is* the answer.
- Mirror their language: "flight deck" not "cockpit", "colleagues" not "staff", "customers" not "passengers" — BA says customers.
