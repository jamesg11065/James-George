# GROUP EXERCISE PLAYBOOK — SPEEDBIRD STAGE 2, WATERSIDE
### James George · Friday · The exercise isn't the task — it's how you communicate

---

## What's Actually Being Scored

Assessors sit outside the circle with a behaviour checklist. Nobody fails for a wrong answer; people fail for how they got there. Your six research points map directly onto the marking sheet:

| Your research point | The competency it scores | The refinement |
|---|---|---|
| Challenge others respectfully | CRM / assertiveness | Challenge the **idea**, never the person — and concede gracefully when the group goes the other way |
| Talk throughout | Engagement / presence | Aim for **steady presence, not constant talking** — a contribution every few minutes; never disappear for five |
| Keep track of time | Situational awareness / workload | Claim it in the first minute, out loud, and actually deliver the updates |
| Only speak if it adds | Communication quality | Never repeat what's been said; add new content, build, structure, or bring someone in |
| Bring people in | Leadership / teamwork | The single highest-scoring behaviour available — do it at least twice |
| Use people's names | Rapport / attention | Write names down at introductions; address every comment to a person, not the table |

Points 2 and 4 pull against each other — resolve it this way: **your default contribution when you have nothing new to add is a process move** (summarise, time-check, bring someone in, test consensus). Process moves always add value, so you can stay present without padding.

## The Opening Two Minutes — Scripted

1. **Introductions**: shake hands, say your name clearly, and **write everyone's name down on a seating map** the moment they say it. You will not remember eight names under pressure; the map means you never fumble one.
2. **Claim the timekeeper role** — exact words: *"I'll monitor the time and keep the group updated."* No one ever objects, and it puts structured contributions on your scoresheet for the whole exercise.
3. **Offer a structure, as a question**: *"We've got 20 minutes — shall we take two to read individually, quickly go round for first thoughts, then discuss and leave the last five to agree the answer?"* Offering structure is leadership; phrasing it as a question means it never reads as dominance.

A go-round at the start also guarantees the quiet people speak once early — which makes it easier to bring them back in later.

## Phrase Bank — Say These, Not Improvised Versions

**Challenging respectfully (idea, not person):**
- "That's a fair point, [name] — my concern with it is [specific reason]. How would we handle that?"
- "I see it slightly differently. [Reasoning]. What am I missing?"
- "Can I push back on that gently? If [X] happens, does the plan still work?"
- Conceding: "That's persuasive — I'm happy to go with [name]'s option." *(Say it once, mean it, move on. Conceding out loud scores; sulking silence doesn't.)*

**Timekeeping updates (deliver at half, 10, 5, and 2 minutes):**
- "Quick time check — we're at halfway, and we've settled [X]; still open is [Y]."
- "Ten minutes left. Shall we start converging on a decision?"
- "Five minutes — I suggest we lock the top three and spend the rest checking everyone's happy."
- "Two minutes — [name], could you summarise what we're submitting so we're all agreed?"

**Bringing people in (do this at least twice):**
- "[Name], we haven't heard from you yet — what's your read on the problem?"
- "[Name], you mentioned [earlier point] — does that change how you'd rank this?"
- "Before we decide, can we quickly hear from anyone who hasn't spoken on this bit?"

**Caging a dominator (redirect, never confront):**
- "That's one strong option — before we settle, can we hear what [quiet name] thinks?"
- "Hold that thought, [name] — I want to make sure we get everyone's view in the time we've got."

**Process moves (your no-new-content default):**
- Summarise: "So far we've agreed [X] and [Y]; the open question is [Z]."
- Test consensus: "Are we all comfortable with that, or does anyone have a concern we should hear?"
- Park a tangent: "Good point — can we park it and come back if there's time?"

**Building on others (worth more than solo brilliance):**
- "Building on [name]'s idea — if we combine it with [Y], we cover both problems."
- "[Name] said something earlier that I think solves this — [repeat it, credited]."

## Likely Formats and How to Play Them

- **Ranking / survival task** (desert, sea, moon — rank items by importance): there IS a reference answer, but it's not what's scored. Play: individual read → go-round → discuss the big disagreements only (don't relitigate every item) → converge. Useful move: "We agree on the top three and bottom three — let's spend our time on the middle."
- **Planning task with constraints** (budget, schedule, resources — e.g. plan an event or allocate limited seats/supplies): someone should hold the constraints. Volunteer *one* job only — you're already timekeeper, so instead flag: "Whatever we pick has to fit the budget line — [name], could you track the numbers as we go?" Delegating a role to someone else is leadership gold.
- **Discussion / consensus topic** (open question, group must agree a position): structure matters most here because there's no natural endpoint. Push the go-round early, summarise the emerging camps by name ("[A] and [B] favour X, [C] prefers Y"), then propose a middle path or a criteria-based way to choose.

In every format, the endgame is the same: **the group must actually finish**. A group that runs out of time without an answer reflects on everyone — your 5- and 2-minute calls are what prevent it.

## Failure Modes — One of These Fails the Exercise

1. **Dominating** — talking over people, repeating your idea louder, fighting after the group has moved on. One bad moment can sink the day.
2. **Disappearing** — five silent minutes reads as disengaged, however hard you're listening. Use a process move to re-enter.
3. **Arguing to win** — being right about a survival item is worth nothing; the marks are in how you disagreed.
4. **Point-scoring off others** — never correct someone in a way that diminishes them; frame it as adding, not fixing.
5. **Ignoring the time** — especially fatal if you claimed timekeeper and then dropped it.
6. **Groupthink shepherding** — racing to agreement without hearing dissent. "Does anyone see a problem with this?" near the end shows you protect against it.

## Rehearsal Plan Before Friday

You can't rehearse a group alone, but you can automate the behaviours:

1. **Say the phrase bank out loud daily** — especially the timekeeper claim, one challenge phrase, and one bring-in phrase, until they're reflexes, not recall.
2. **Run one scenario per evening against a 15-minute timer** — argue both sides aloud, practise the updates at 10, 5, and 2 minutes:
   - *Desert survival*: your vehicle broke down 100km from help; rank 10 items (water, mirror, map, coat, pistol, salt tablets, torch, compass, plastic sheet, book of edible plants).
   - *Sea survival*: yacht sinking; rank 10 items (sextant, shaving mirror, water canister, mosquito net, food rations, maps, seat cushion, oil can, radio, rope).
   - *Planning*: your group must plan a charity open day with £500, one weekend, six volunteers — decide the event and the budget split.
3. **Recruit one person** (family, friend) for a 15-minute mock where they play a dominator — practise the redirect phrase live once and it'll be available on Friday.
4. **On the day**: seating map + names first, timekeeper claim second, structure offer third. After that the playbook runs itself.

## The Mindset Sentence

Walk in with this: **"My job isn't to have the best answer — it's to make this group better at reaching one."** Every behaviour above follows from it, and it happens to be a precise description of the professional First Officer they're selecting for.
